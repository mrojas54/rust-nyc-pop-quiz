Pop Quiz — the takes

Stage 2 of the Tone arc, tone-prototype. Three directions, each resting on a different core assumption about where the segment actually happens — not one direction in three colourways. Swap the headline between any two and you would still tell them apart.

Every take runs on the real verified batch from mvp/2026-08-12/: real sources, real distractors, the real long explanations, real receipt facts, and q3's answer at E exactly as slot_for_day() drew it. The room numbers come from sequence/research/06-attendees.md. Nothing is tidied up to make a layout look better — that is the whole point of prototyping against real data.

The pick is the start of the design, not the end. Whichever direction you like, we iterate it together until you love it — many rounds is normal here, because this is the cheapest the design will ever be to change.

Revised 2026-08-14 — being wrong is the ordinary thing Client testimony: the feedback Rust NYC gets, surfaced through Women in Rust, is that everything is very intimidating; people aren't allowed to be wrong. The first cut of these takes did the opposite of that on every surface that mattered — it put a red ✗ on your own answer. That is gone. Three changes across all three directions:
  • The split comes before the answer. You find out you are not alone before you find out you were wrong. The order is the whole mechanism.
  • Nothing marks a person as wrong. The answer gets a ✓; your choice gets a count of how many people read it the same way.
  • The explanation spends its middle paragraph on the popular wrong answer — what 24 of us saw in [1, 2, 3], and why it is a reasonable thing to have seen.

The three directions

A · Hands

The room's voice beats its phones. No participant app exists. A projector and the host's own phone, and the room votes out loud one letter at a time. Stories B1, B5 and most of B6 evaporate; the build shrinks to a fraction.

B · Phone-first

Commitment is private. Anonymity is what makes people willing to be wrong in front of colleagues, so the phone carries the code, the answer and the reveal. The most expensive direction — and the only one that needs the 200-concurrent write burst.

C · Projector-first, phone as buzzer

Fifty people reading the same nine lines is the event. The code lives on the big screen and nowhere else; the phone is five letters. The explanation is spoken rather than read. Includes a back-of-the-room test you can actually run.

The two surfaces that don't fork

Review surface

Stories A4, A6, A7 — reading a batch, affirming explanations under a blocking gate, watching the reserve. One take, not three: no core-assumption fork, and it is a tool for one person.

The Discord seam

Discord's own surface mocked alongside ours, plus a one-page seam diagram. Five sign-in cases including the one that matters — a server Administrator who still cannot host.
What I'd like you to do with these
  • Drive them, don't skim them. Click through a whole segment in each — start to release.
  • In C, hit "6 metres back" while the question is live. That is the test the room runs every time, and it is the one thing that decides whether C is real.
  • In A, run the hand-count. It is the only direction where you are the measuring instrument, and five minutes is not long.
  • Try the lights down toggle on A and C. AC-80 says if a dim room wins, the brand's "no dark mode in v1" is what gets amended — so this is a real decision, not a preview.
  • In the review surface, watch the clock in the corner. AC-21 targets 15 minutes for a batch and nobody has ever measured it.
